### 1. The Social Science Standard: "Everything You Can Hear"
In political science, qualitative sociology, and discourse analysis, the scenario you described—a citizen ignoring the gavel and talking over a council member—is not considered "noise" to be filtered out. It is considered a **highly salient data point**. 

When analyzing public comments, researchers are usually measuring civic engagement, polarization, and public sentiment. A commenter who calmly yields their time at 3:00 has a very different emotional baseline than a commenter who passionately yells until their microphone is cut at 4:15. 

The standard is to analyze **everything that is audible**. If you only analyze the "officially allowed time," you are artificially censoring your dataset and missing the peak emotional expression of the most fired-up citizens. The interruption itself (and the citizen's reaction to it) is vital context for the LLM to accurately score the sentiment and confidence.

### 2. How "Exclusive Mode" Fails Here
If you use `exclusive_speaker_diarization`, Pyannote is mathematically forced to assign every millisecond of audio to exactly one person. 

When the council member gently says "Please wrap up" while the commenter is loudly ranting, the model relies on acoustic energy (loudness and vocal dominance) to pick the "main" speaker. 
*   **The likely result:** The model will assign the audio to the loud commenter, completely erasing the council member's interruption from the diarization timeline. 
*   **The worst-case result:** The model gets confused by the overlapping frequencies and rapidly flickers back and forth between `Speaker_A` and `Speaker_B` every half-second, shredding your audio into unusable, choppy 500-millisecond slices.

### 3. How the Pipeline Will Behave (The Overlap Problem)
If you turn *off* exclusive mode (which you should), Pyannote will correctly output that two people are speaking at the same time:
*   `[03:00 - 04:15] Speaker_Commenter`
*   `[03:05 - 03:10] Speaker_CouncilMember`

However, this creates a downstream problem for your transcription step. If you slice the audio based on Pyannote's timestamps and send it to Whisper, what does Whisper do with overlapping voices?

Whisper is a single-channel transcriber. It does not separate voices. When it hears two people talking at once, it usually does one of three things:
1.  It transcribes only the loudest voice.
2.  It transcribes both voices sequentially, mashing them into one sentence (e.g., *"And another thing we need to fix please wrap up your time is the potholes on Main Street!"*).
3.  It gets confused and skips the overlapping section entirely.

### The Recommended Solution: The "Contiguous Block" Strategy
To capture the full reality of the meeting without breaking your pipeline, you should abandon the idea of perfectly isolating the commenter's voice. Instead, use Pyannote to identify the **boundaries of the interaction**.

Here is how you should design this step:

**1. Use Standard Diarization (No Exclusive Mode)**
Let Pyannote flag the overlapping speech. 

**2. Merge Overlapping Timestamps in Python**
Write a simple logic rule in your Python script: If a "Commenter" is speaking, and a "Council Member" interrupts them, **do not slice the audio**. Treat the entire block from when the commenter starts to when they finally stop as one single, contiguous audio file. 

**3. Let Whisper Transcribe the Mess**
Send that entire 4-minute chunk to Whisper `large-v3`. Whisper is remarkably good at transcribing the dominant voice, and will likely capture the commenter's rant and perhaps the council member's interruptions mixed in.

**4. Let the LLM Sort It Out**
This is where your local Llama 3 model shines. LLMs are incredibly good at reading messy, overlapping transcripts and understanding the context. You simply adjust your system prompt to expect this:

> *"You are analyzing a public comment from a city council meeting. The transcript may contain interruptions from council members telling the speaker their time is up. Ignore the council members' interruptions. Focus only on the citizen's statements to determine their sentiment, confidence, and to generate a summary."*

By doing this, you preserve the raw, emotional reality of the meeting—capturing the full length of the citizen's grievance—while relying on the LLM's reasoning capabilities to filter out the procedural interruptions, rather than relying on brittle audio-slicing math.
