# AI Data Center Public Comment Analysis: Human-Identified Themes

## Context

This document defines the four thematic categories identified by human researchers through exploratory thematic analysis of Lancaster, PA city council public comments (July–November 2025) regarding a proposed AI data center development.

**Analytical stance:** The researchers did not assess the factual accuracy of speakers' claims. Comments are treated as *situated narratives* — the focus is on how community members articulate perceptions of risk, expectations of responsibility, and core values in the face of municipal governance. In other words: how residents narrate, explain, and connect topical concerns to other issues.

**Task for the LLM:** Given a public comment from a municipal meeting about an AI data center, identify which of the four themes below the comment belongs to (a comment may belong to multiple themes). Reproduce the human analysts' categorization logic.

---

## Theme 1: Municipally Managed Resources and Utilities

**Definition:** Comments that frame the data center project as a strain on Lancaster's municipally managed resources — electricity/electric infrastructure, water supply, waste management, and the city's budgetary capacity to handle increased usage.

### Sub-theme 1a: Electricity supply, quality, and cost
Speakers express concern that the grid cannot absorb the added load, or that the cost of expanding and maintaining infrastructure will fall on residents.

> *"There will be no value to having the proposed data centers here other than ridding ourselves of the problem of an empty warehouse."* — Speaker 1

> *"It's not just the cost to increase the power grid, it's the cost to maintain a much, much, much larger power grid."* — Speaker 5

### Sub-theme 1b: Water supply, quality, and cost
Speakers raise concerns about water availability and quality, particularly in the context of already-documented contamination issues (Lancaster had elevated PFAS levels as of March 2024) and the large volumes of water data centers consume for cooling.

> *"Our water is already questionable. Our energy is already questionable. Who else's rent is going up, right? My rent's up."* — Speaker 54

> *"Potable water, where it's available, especially in a community like ours, that is dependent on rainfall to dilute the — what are we calling them? PFOs in our water? It's critical that we save every drop of potable water we have, so they shouldn't be going to just cooling uses."* — Speaker 67

### Sub-theme 1c: Electronic waste and end-of-life liability
Speakers warn about what happens when the hardware becomes obsolete — concern that the developer will walk away and leave the city with decommissioned equipment and a degraded site.

> *(Paraphrase)* Speaker 68 pointed out a critical oversight in the project's lifecycle regarding e-waste and disposal of decommissioned parts, worrying developers would simply "throw the kids on the roof and walk away."

**Synthesis:** This theme frames AI infrastructure as a long-term burden on shared municipal utilities — not just price, but connected to broader well-being and fiscal responsibility.

## Theme 2: Municipal Process

**Definition:** Comments that frame the unfolding data center project as illustrative of unmet expectations with locally elected officials — specifically around *how* decisions were made, *how* knowledge was shared, and *what* accountability looks like. Includes demands for community-led monitoring, expressions of information asymmetry, distrust of officials, and moral condemnation of the process.

### Sub-theme 2a: Demand for community-led oversight and participation
Speakers assert a desire for direct, ongoing involvement in monitoring and regulating construction and operations — not just a one-time vote.

> *"Who is going to be measuring did they expand past the existing footprint? And it has to be us. It has to be the city."* — Speaker 16

> *"I would certainly like to stay on top of where this work is coming from and where it's going."* — Speaker 50

### Sub-theme 2b: Information asymmetry and lack of transparency
Speakers express that the community was kept in the dark while decisions were being made — the gap between the land deed purchase and public awareness was a primary point of contention.

> *"People want to believe in their leaders. But these days, especially trust is fragile and transparency is the only way forward."* — Speaker 49

### Sub-theme 2c: Distrust, accusation, and moral condemnation
As the process escalated, speakers moved from expressing disappointment to explicit accusation of bad faith by officials.

> *"One individual, the mayor, went behind the backs of many people to sell out our community for DICTA."* — Speaker 7D

**Synthesis:** Comments in this theme centered on the initial zoning determination, adequacy of public notice, and the legitimacy of the community benefits agreement process.

## Theme 3: Health and Well-being

**Definition:** Comments expressing concern about the direct and indirect effects of data center operations on residents' physical health and quality of life — including noise pollution, air quality, documented health conditions, and the argument that no financial benefit can offset health harms.

### Sub-theme 3a: Noise pollution
Speakers cite decibel levels from air-cooled facilities as a serious concern for nearby residents.

> *"Air cooling could mean significant noise pollution for anyone living within a quarter mile of it. The decibels could reach well over those considered safe for humans near air-cooled data centers."* — Speaker (July 22nd)

### Sub-theme 3b: Air quality and outdoor quality of life
Speakers connect declining air quality to the loss of everyday outdoor activities — parks, concerts, green space — citing these as core parts of Lancaster life.

> *"Who here? Raise your hand. Has gone to a concert in the park in the summertime... Like we were, we really use every single resource, every single green space that's available to us here to get fresh air, to get away from the chaos of the city."* — Speaker (August 6th)

### Sub-theme 3c: Direct health consequences
Speakers cite media reports and examples from other states (Maryland, New York, New Jersey, Delaware) to argue that data centers produce greenhouse gases and compounds linked to respiratory, digestive, and cardiovascular disease. Specific conditions mentioned include migraines, asthma, heart attacks, and sleep issues.

> *"We're seeing that now as more and more of these facilities make national headlines for churning out enormous amounts of greenhouse gases and other compounds inextricably linked to cancer and cardiovascular and respiratory diseases."*

### Sub-theme 3d: Health vs. economic benefit — the trade-off frame
Speakers explicitly reject the framing that financial benefits justify health risks, using "trade," "exchange," and direct statements of refusal.

> *"I can't eat money and I can't breathe money and nobody else here can."*

> *"There's no amount of financial benefit offered by these data centers that could conceivably offset the corresponding detriment to public health and welfare."* — Speaker (October 6th)

**Synthesis:** Speakers treat their bodies and outdoor environment as non-negotiable; they use evidence from other communities and media to preemptively argue the health costs are real and unacceptable.

## Theme 4: Power Dynamics and Inequality

**Definition:** Comments that highlight the uneven distribution of costs and benefits — who bears the risks, who profits, and how existing systems of wealth and power shape outcomes. Includes concern for non-human entities and vulnerable populations, challenges to the job-creation narrative, and connections between AI infrastructure and historical patterns of extraction and neglect.

### Sub-theme 4a: Non-human and vulnerable populations
Speakers extend their concern beyond themselves to wildlife, children, and future generations.

> *"Have they done any studies on animal impact? There are many, many, many migratory birds that are in Longs Park. Are they affected?"* — Speaker (July 22nd)

> *"I don't have any children, but I care about your kids, and I'm not willing to sign them up to be a real-time experiment of how much poison air you can breathe."* — Speaker (future work example cited by researchers)

### Sub-theme 4b: Challenging the jobs narrative
Speakers push back against the developer promise of job creation by pointing to unmet existing needs — housing, infrastructure — as more legitimate priorities.

> *"I think a majority of the people in this town want to see you get jobs, but for housing projects. We want to talk about housing projects and developing a community."* — Speaker (November 20th)

### Sub-theme 4c: Uneven distribution of cost and benefit / historical patterns
Speakers draw on historical patterns of wealth, power, and extraction to argue that the community will inherit the costs while the benefits flow elsewhere. Fossil fuel use and energy costs are invoked as predictable harms based on prior experience.

*(Paraphrased throughout)*: Residents emphasize the misalignment between benefits and harms — economic gains accrue to developers while environmental and health costs fall disproportionately on residents, echoing prior patterns of industrial development.

**Synthesis:** Speakers are not simply reacting to a single project. They are reasoning across environmental, economic, and ethical scales and producing knowledge about AI's value chain and its uneven distribution of cost and benefit.
